# Calibration: what the corpus protects

*What a conversion's calibration data does and does not protect, measured on
Qwen3.8-27B at 4 bpw between 2026-09-22 and 2026-09-27, and the conversational
calibration mix that came out of it; then a small proxy (Ornith-1.5-9B) and an
uncalibrated reference, 2026-09-28 to 2026-10-01, which settled how the traces behind
calibration and eval should be generated. The instrument side — the conversational qbench
slices, and what `template: render` was actually measuring — is in
[qbench.md](qbench.md) "Conversational trace slices".*

**The short version.** EXL3's quantizer holds error down in the activation directions its
calibration exercises, and nowhere else. The bundled corpus is raw text, so it protects
raw text and the model's own chat, and leaves external text sitting inside a
conversation unprotected — which is exactly where a quantized model's quality varied
from draw to draw, and where a pure self-chat corpus (`sc_trace.py`) lost 5x. A
calibration mix that puts the same corpus into conversational form — pasted documents,
tool results, short tool loops, a little of the model's own voice — cuts excess KLD
12-13% on real user prompts (WildChat), 17-18% on turboderp's own independent eval trace,
14-29% on constructed conversational slices whose scaffolding calibration never saw, and
costs nothing on raw text.

Ornith-1.5-9B, which shares Qwen3.8's architecture, reproduces the chat-tier gain and
serves as a proxy for tuning the mix, though not for the agentic draw variance. An
uncalibrated quant (`convert.py --uncalibrated`) is a steady reference at 1.7x the
default calibration's error, and as the generator of a calibration trace it is as good as
a calibrated quant. The policy that came out of it: calibration traces from an
uncalibrated 8bpw, eval traces from bf16 (FP8 where bf16 does not fit).

## The result: a conversational calibration mix (2026-09-25)

Built with `ctx_trace.py --docs cal --cal_out` in the `yeasah/exllamav3` fork, passed to
`convert.py --cal_data`. Composition, as 250 rows x 2048 tokens:

| slice | rows | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| raw | 65 | rows of the bundled default mix, sampled across all its sources in proportion |
| context | 88 | a corpus document pasted into a user turn under an anchoring request ("Summarize this", "What does this code do?"), or returned as a tool result, then the model's own answer |
| loop | 62 | a scripted 2-3 round tool loop (`read_file`, `fetch_url`, `search`) whose results are corpus documents of the matching kind, then the model's answer |
| self | 23 | the model's own voice on `sc_trace.py`'s prompts, excluding the ones the eval slice uses |
| random | 12 | the default mix's random-token rows |

Documents come from the bundled corpus (c4, wiki, technical, code) plus Wikipedia
(20231101, row group 0) in 13 languages. Answers were generated by turboderp's 6.00bpw
with thinking on at medium effort — 242 conversations, 286k context and 177k response
tokens, about 2.5 hours on two 5060 Tis. The shares are provisional and untuned.

Scored against turboderp's published plain 4bpw, as the ratio of excess KLD (below 1 is
better), with 90% row-bootstrap intervals, across three conversion draws. The eval traces
share nothing with calibration beyond the model: documents, own-voice prompts, anchor
phrasings, tool names and schemas, and task wording are all disjoint, and each eval
conversation runs with one of four thinking settings where calibration used one
([qbench.md](qbench.md) "Conversational trace slices").

| eval | mixed calibration | td SC 4bpw | our default-cal draws |
|---|---|---|---|
| **real user prompts (WildChat, 60)** | **0.87-0.88** [0.85, 0.91] | 0.92 | 0.98-1.01 |
| **turboderp's own eval trace (24)** | **0.82-0.83** | 0.75 | 0.99-1.01 |
| **real agent sessions (Open-SWE-Traces, 40)** | **0.73-0.89** [0.68, 0.95] | 1.06 | **0.94-1.23** |
| agentic (2 captured Hermes sessions, 19 turns) | 0.85-0.96 | 1.03 | 0.98-1.22 |
| document in user turn | 0.82-0.85 | 1.00 | 1.01-1.07 |
| document as tool result | 0.71-0.74 | 0.95 | 0.93-0.99 |
| tool loop | 0.76-0.77 | 0.98 | 0.99-1.05 |
| multilingual documents | 0.84-0.86 | 1.00 | 0.95-1.05 |
| own voice | 0.85-0.87 | **0.69** | 0.98-1.02 |

The first three rows are the independent tier: prompts and scaffolding nobody here wrote.
The rest are constructed by `ctx_trace.py` and serve to locate an effect, not to claim one.

- **Raw text is unchanged:** excess KLD 0.0077-0.0079 against 0.0078 for turboderp's and
  0.0076-0.0080 for the default-calibration draws.
- **Multilingual improved rather than paying for the new formats**: 14-16% on held-out
  Wikipedia in 13 languages. Predominantly non-English calibration rows went from 6%
  (default) to 9%, non-Latin letters from 2.3% to 4.7%. European languages are not better
  covered than before — a weak signal, but the obvious place to look when the shares are
  tuned.
- **All three draws land within a few percent of one another** on every eval but one: real
  coding-agent sessions, where they spread 0.73-0.89 — and the default draws spread 0.94-1.23.
  Long agent contexts (large tool outputs, source files, another model's action history) are
  the regime the mix covers least: its loop slice is two or three scripted rounds over corpus
  documents, not a real multi-step session. That is where the remaining draw variance lives,
  and the case for an agentic calibration slice drawn from the disjoint half of the same
  dataset (TODO.md `calibration-mix`).
- **turboderp's SC keeps its home ground** — the model's own chat, generated by the same
  6bpw that produced SC's calibration trace — and ties or loses elsewhere; on real user
  prompts it is 8% better than plain, the mix 12-13%.

**Separating the scaffolding moved less than expected (2026-09-27).** The first round of
eval traces shared calibration's anchor phrasings, tool definitions and task wording,
and ran at calibration's single thinking setting. Against those, the mix scored 0.73-0.77
on user-turn documents, 0.67-0.72 on tool results, 0.75-0.79 on loops, 0.77-0.82 on
multilingual and 0.83-0.85 on own voice. With everything split, user-turn and
multilingual lost about 8 points each — some of that gain was fit to the anchors — while
tool results, loops and own voice barely moved. The tool-result gain, the one most
suspected of being scaffolding fit, is the most robust. SC's small first-round edge on
user-turn documents (0.89) vanished (1.00) the same way.

**What this licenses.** One model, one bitrate, three draws. It does not yet say anything
about 2-3 bpw at 27B, where the default calibration's draws were furthest from smooth, or
about other models; on the Ornith-9B proxy the gain holds at 2 and 3 bpw (0.92 and 0.89 on
WildChat, single draws — "An uncalibrated reference" below). gemma-4-12B is the obvious
second case: it is the only other model found whose default-calibration draws spread
widely under render (three draws, 2.0x in mean and 3.2x in median KLD), where Qwen3-4B
(1.16x) and Qwen3-0.6B (1.06x at 4 bpw, 1.21x even at 2 bpw) behave ordinarily.

## A small proxy: Ornith-1.5-9B (2026-09-28)

Iterating on the mix at 27B costs most of a day per variant, so the same pipeline was
repeated on Ornith-1.5-9B, the one small model on hand with Qwen3.8's architecture
(`Qwen3_5ForConditionalGeneration`, 3:1 gated-delta-net/attention, 16 key heads). The
recipe was identical: the same shares, seeds, document pools and own-voice exclusions,
with the calibration and all seven eval traces regenerated from a default-calibration
Ornith 6bpw (the calibration's input side matched the 27B's token for token, 286,390
tokens). Three default draws and three mix draws were converted at 4 bpw with
`EXL3_SEED_IDX_OFFSET` 0, 100000 and 200000; the table below is from those, and a fourth
mix draw (300000), added for the generator test, widened the mix ranges on swe to
0.83-0.91, multilingual to 0.89-0.92 and user-turn documents to 0.84-0.85, and left the
rest unchanged.

Ornith has no published 4bpw, so both models are scored here against **the mean of their
own default draws** (the 27B's five), with 90% row-bootstrap intervals on the mix mean:

| eval | 9B default draws | 9B mix | 27B default draws | 27B mix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **real user prompts (WildChat, 60)** | 0.99-1.01 | **0.89-0.91** [0.87, 0.92] | 0.98-1.01 | 0.88-0.89 [0.87, 0.90] |
| **real agent sessions (swe, 40)** | 0.97-1.03 | 0.88-0.91 [0.86, 0.93] | **0.86-1.14** | 0.67-0.81 [0.69, 0.78] |
| document in user turn | 1.00 | 0.84 | 0.98-1.03 | 0.79-0.82 |
| document as tool result | 0.98-1.02 | 0.80-0.82 | 0.97-1.03 | 0.74-0.77 |
| multilingual documents | 0.99-1.01 | 0.89-0.90 | 0.96-1.05 | 0.84-0.87 |
| tool loop | 0.98-1.02 | 0.77-0.82 | 0.98-1.04 | 0.75-0.76 |
| own voice | 0.99-1.02 | 0.86-0.90 | 0.99-1.03 | 0.86-0.87 |
| **raw text (openwebtext, 50 x 2048)** | 0.98-1.01 | **1.03-1.07** [1.03, 1.06] | — | ~1.00 (0.0077-0.0079 vs 0.0076-0.0080) |

- **What carries over:** the chat-tier gain almost exactly (WildChat, own voice), and
  the ordering of the constructed slices — tool results and loops gain most,
  multilingual least — at 3-6 points less gain throughout.
- **What does not:** the default calibration's draw lottery. Ornith's default draws agree
  within 3% on real agent sessions, where the 27B's spread 0.86-1.14, and the mix's swe gain
  is a third of the 27B's. As with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-0.6B under render, the small model's
  default draws do not show the variance, so the proxy cannot stand in for the
  agentic-slice question. Its *mix* draws do spread, though: four of them read 0.79-0.93
  on swe and 0.79-0.89 on tool results against bf16-generated evals (2026-10-01), so
  comparing calibrations on the proxy needs several draws per arm.
- **What is new: a 3-7% cost on raw text.** At 27B the mix left raw text unchanged. Two
  readings, not yet separated: the smaller model needs raw coverage the mix dilutes (65 raw
  rows, against the default corpus's ~238), or the 27B carries a cost of this size that
  its 10-row raw control could not resolve. Ornith's own thinking is also more verbose:
  99 of 242 calibration responses hit the 1024-token cap (56 before `</think>`), against
  73 for the 27B, so its calibration holds less answer text. Either way the cost is
  accepted: raw web text framed as nothing matches any real use, so raw text is a guard
  with a tolerance, not something to buy back at the expense of a real slice.

Cost per full pass on the local 2x5060 Ti host: 49 minutes for a calibrated 6bpw
generator (about 30 for an uncalibrated 8bpw), 58 for the calibration trace, 75 for the
eval traces, 47-50 minutes per 4 bpw draw alone or 51-57 for two run concurrently (swap
absorbs the heads' memory peak), and 2 hours of scoring for six arms over eight slices.
A shares variant needs no new eval traces, and since `ctx_trace.py --repack` (2026-10-01)
no new calibration trace either when it only shrinks generated slices: generation
over-provisions each slice by 1.25x, and a repack at the original shares reproduces the
generated pack byte for byte. So a variant is a minute of packing, an hour for a
concurrent pair of draws, and an hour of scoring. Two things still need generation:
growing a slice past its spare material (own voice has none — its 23 rows already
exhaust the prompt pool left after the eval's exclusions), and anything about the
documents themselves, such as `ml_frac` or which languages they come from.

Project files: `/home/bulk/ypell/quant_work/_orn9_mix/` (`ratios.py` computes both
tables).

## An uncalibrated reference (2026-09-29)

`convert.py --uncalibrated` (fork) quantizes every layer through the identity-Hessian
path that MTP heads already take. Two uses were in view: a reference that exists for
every model at every bitrate, for scaling card results and measuring what calibration is
worth at all; and a calibration-neutral generator for the traces a conversion's own
calibration is built from, in place of an existing quant or a quant made with some
arbitrary calibration. On Ornith-1.5-9B, excess KLD (two uncalibrated draws per bitrate
except one at 5 bpw; one calibrated draw at 2 and 3 bpw, three at 4):

| bpw | WildChat: default | mix | uncalibrated | unc / default | raw text: unc / default | mix / default |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.295 | 0.272 | 0.497-0.514 | 1.71 | 1.77 | 0.92 |
| 3 | 0.071 | 0.063 | 0.118-0.122 | 1.68 | 1.80 | 0.89 |
| 4 | 0.0196-0.0200 | 0.0175-0.0180 | 0.0338-0.0341 | 1.72 | 1.85 | 0.90 |
| 5 | — | — | 0.0100 | — | — | — |
| 6 | 0.0013 | — | 0.0027 | 1.98 | 2.49 | — |

- **As a denominator it is steady.** Uncalibrated sits at 1.7x the default calibration's
  excess across 2-4 bpw on WildChat (1.8x on raw text), and its two draws agree within
  1-3% there. On swe the ratio reads 2.37, 1.50, 1.55 — single calibrated draws at 2 and 3
  bpw, likely draw noise rather than shape. At 6 bpw the excess is within 1-3x of the
  bf16 noise floor (0.0008), so ratios there are unreliable.
- **What calibration is worth:** the default corpus removes ~42% of uncalibrated error,
  the mix ~47%, roughly constant from 2 to 4 bpw.
- **Normalizing does not wash out calibration differences.** Dividing every arm at a
  bitrate by the same number preserves their ratios: mix / default stays 0.89-0.92 at
  every bitrate. Normalized, mix and default read 0.52-0.54 and 0.58-0.59 — visually
  compressed on a linear axis, identical on a log one.
- **As a generator it is as good as a calibrated one.** Uncalibrated 6bpw has twice the
  calibrated 6bpw's excess (0.0027 vs 0.0013; perplexity 1.4885 vs 1.4827, bf16 1.4858),
  still 7x below any 4 bpw arm. Tested directly: the mix trace regenerated from an
  uncalibrated 8bpw (same seed and arguments; identical inputs, 100 vs 99 truncated
  responses), four draws against four from the original trace, scored with a bootstrap
  over draws as well as rows (below).

| eval | uncalibrated-generator mix / calibrated-generator mix [90%] |
|---|---|
| **real user prompts (WildChat)** | 0.996 [0.97, 1.02] |
| **real agent sessions (swe)** | 0.997 [0.90, 1.08] |
| document in user turn | 1.001 [0.97, 1.03] |
| document as tool result | 1.006 [0.96, 1.05] |
| own voice | 1.007 [0.98, 1.04] |
| multilingual documents | 0.962 [0.91, 1.01] |
| tool loop, eval generated by the calibrated 6bpw | 1.049 [1.01, 1.10] |
| tool loop, eval generated by the uncalibrated 8bpw | **0.990** [0.95, 1.03] |
| raw text | 0.996 [0.97, 1.02] |

  The one apparent loss was the eval, not the generator. Every eval trace's assistant
  turns come from a generator, and the loop slice — the one most made of the generator's
  own tokens (tool calls and answers) — was regenerated by each candidate and by bf16,
  then scored with all eight mix draws (excess relative to default, 90% intervals of
  ±3-4 points per cell):

| loop eval generated by | original mix | uncalibrated-generator mix | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated 6bpw (the original mix's generator) | 0.799 | 0.839 | 1.049 [1.01, 1.10] |
| uncalibrated 8bpw (the other's generator) | 0.830 | 0.822 | 0.990 [0.95, 1.03] |
| **bf16** | **0.803** | **0.811** | **1.009** [0.97, 1.05] |

  On bf16's text the two tie, and each scores about as it does on its own generator's
  text. The distortion is a penalty on *another* quant generator's text, not a bonus on
  one's own: one quant's sampled text carries quirks that a calibration built from a
  different quant's text does not cover. Two generators that differ by ~0.002 excess KLD
  per token still differ measurably over a sampled trajectory — on the order of nats per
  thousand-token response, which is why same-seed generations diverge after a median of
  32 tokens. **Policy: a conversion's calibration trace is generated from an uncalibrated
  8bpw** — no extra calibrated quant to make or fetch, and no arbitrary calibration
  upstream of the one being built — **and eval traces from bf16, or FP8 where bf16 does
  not fit; never from an EXL3 quant out of the pipeline being measured.** A different
  model is not needed: the scored turn must stay in the model's own voice, which is what
  use scores (scoring another model's turn is what `template: render` did), and that
  shared voice is legitimate credit for a calibration that covers it.

- **What this means for the Qwen3.8 numbers.** There, calibration (mix and SC) and the
  constructed eval slices were all generated by turboderp's 6bpw. By the table above, a
  mix scored on its own generator's text reads about as it would on bf16's, so the
  mix-versus-default gains should stand; comparisons between two calibrations from
  *different* generators would not, and none were made there. The independent tier is
  where this matters least — WildChat showed no generator effect at all — which is one
  more reason the card composite is built from it alone.

**It has a draw lottery of its own (2026-10-03).** Two draws per bitrate had suggested the
uncalibrated ladder was steadier than calibrated ones (0.9-1.4% apart at 4 bpw), which fit a
mechanism: with no Hessian, error is spread evenly, so no draw can be unlucky in an uncovered
direction. Eight draws per bitrate say otherwise. Relative standard deviation across draws,
on the bf16-generated evals:

| | draws | WildChat | swe | composite |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| uncalibrated 2 bpw | 8 | 2.1% | 2.4% | 1.8% |
| default 2 bpw | 3 | 0.7% | 5.7% | 1.4% |
| uncalibrated 4 bpw | 8 | 1.9% | 3.8% | 1.5% |
| default 4 bpw | 3 | 1.0% | 2.1% | 0.8% |
| mix 4 bpw | 4 | 1.4% | 7.0% | 1.7% |

Evenly spread error still lands on a fixed set of inputs differently each draw, about as much
as a calibrated set's does. So a single uncalibrated rung is no steadier a reference than a
single calibrated one; its case is neutrality and availability. As the denominator of a
comparison between ladders its noise is common-mode -- every trace at a given size divides by
the same value, so their order and gaps survive and only each curve's shape across bitrates
picks up ~1.5-2% -- so one draw per rung is enough there. The mix's wide swe spread stands
out (7.0%): the agentic regime again.

Cost: an uncalibrated conversion runs at half the per-layer time of a calibrated one
(31 vs 64 s) in 1.6 GB of host memory rather than 11.5. Two calibrated conversions run
concurrently through swap at 51-57 minutes each against 47-50 alone — 1.8x throughput,
with the heads' memory peak absorbed. The flag's first large-vocab head exposed a
`fallback_quant` defect that upstream shares ([upstream.md](upstream.md)).

The first full `quant.py` run (traces, mix calibration, bf16 eval traces; one draw per
bitrate) scored against the published default-calibrated `yeasah/Ornith-1.5-9B-exl3` on
the same traces, as excess KLD relative to it:

| bpw | composite, at equal size | worst of seven eval slices, same bitrate |
|---|---|---|
| 2.00 | 0.92 | 0.93 (own voice) |
| 3.00 | 0.89 | 0.92 (agent sessions) |
| 4.00 | 0.89 | 0.92 (own voice) |
| 5.00 | 0.86 | 0.91 (own voice) |
| 6.00 | 0.87 | 0.96 (agent sessions) |

The gain holds at every bitrate, and no slice is worse anywhere. Single draws on both

## Eval sampling does not reorder calibrations (2026-10-04)

Every trace until now was sampled with exllamav3's default (temperature 0.8, min-p 0.08),
which is no model's recommendation. WildChat regenerated from bf16 with Ornith-1.5-9B's
general-task setting minus its presence penalty (temperature 1.0, top-k 20, top-p 0.95;
see TODO `trace-sampling` for why the penalty waits), and the same 4 bpw draws rescored:

| | default sampling | model sampling |
|---|---|---|
| bf16 perplexity on its own text | 1.463 | 1.577 |
| mix / default | 0.906 | 0.883 |
| default / uncalibrated | 0.588 | 0.589 |
| mix / uncalibrated | 0.533 | 0.520 |

Hotter text raises every group's excess KLD 2-4%, and leaves the standings where they were;
the mix's margin, if anything, widens (about 2 standard errors at 3-4 draws a side). The
calibration data built with default sampling stays; eval traces use the model's own sampling
from here on, and absolute numbers from the two are not mixed.

## An agentic calibration slice, on the proxy (2026-10-05)

Real coding-agent sessions as a calibration slice: Open-SWE-Traces' mini-swe-agent sessions
generated by Qwen3.8-27B on swe-rebench-v2 (all eight languages), with every repository in the
eval `swe` slice excluded, each session regenerated at a cut by the calibration generator and
packed as one or two full 2048-token windows ending at the response. Shares raw .20, ctx .30,
loop .15, agent .20, self .10, random .05 (50 rows from 25 sessions), against the control
mix's 25/35/25/10/5, four 4 bpw draws each, eval traces from bf16 at Ornith's own sampling:

| eval slice | control / default | agent / default | draw sd, control → agent | agent / control [90%] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **wild** | 0.880 | 0.907 | 1.1% → 1.0% | **1.031** [1.007, 1.054] |
| **swe** (40) | 0.869 | 0.856 | 5.9% → 7.4% | 0.985 [0.900, 1.077] |
| ctx_user | 0.826 | 0.844 | 1.3% → 2.0% | 1.021 [0.987, 1.054] |
| ctx_tool | 0.820 | 0.827 | 3.8% → 2.0% | 1.009 [0.965, 1.059] |
| ctx_ml | 0.843 | 0.907 | 3.4% → 2.7% | **1.075** [1.022, 1.134] |
| loop | 0.822 | 0.837 | 1.5% → 1.3% | 1.017 [0.982, 1.051] |
| self | 0.874 | 0.906 | 0.8% → 1.5% | **1.037** [1.007, 1.069] |

Intervals bootstrap draws and rows together. The composite goes from 0.878 of default to
0.897, 2.2% worse; swe does not measurably move and its draw spread does not tighten. Two
things were changed at once — agent's share came out of ctx, loop and raw — so this does not
say whether the sessions are poor calibration or the dilution cost more than they added;
ctx_ml, the slice that only ctx's multilingual documents cover, losing most suggests the
dilution. `CAL_SHARES` went back to the control mix.

Why so little to add: the control mix already carries what the sessions mostly are. The
`code` documents in ctx and loop come from the bundled `code.utf8`, which is not Python only
(of its 56 24 kB chunks, by dominant syntax: JS/TS 18, Python 15, C/C++ 10, shell 4, Go 4,
Rust 3, PHP 2) and carries HTML, markdown, JSON and some CSS and YAML; loop already has the
outward shape of tool use. The control mix improves swe 13% over default with no agent data
at all. What the corpus lacks almost entirely, by a pattern count: SQL, LaTeX, MathML, XML and
diffs. Split by language, the agent arm's swe ratios lean toward the languages `code.utf8` is
thin in (Go 0.92, Java 0.92, Rust 0.75, TypeScript 0.72; Python and JS 1.02) — but Rust and
TypeScript are 376 scored tokens between them.

And the instrument was weak: the 40-row swe slice is 7.7k scored tokens (median 79 per
row), and its 3 worst rows carry half the excess, 8 rows three quarters — which is where the
6-7% draw spread comes from. A 200-session swe eval (seed 2, the agent slice's repositories
excluded; 23k scored tokens, its 3 worst rows 12% of the excess) re-scored the same draws:

| | default | control | agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean excess KLD | 0.02239 | 0.01999 | 0.01888 |
| draws | 0.0231, 0.0225, 0.0216 | 0.0205, 0.0195, 0.0198, 0.0202 | 0.0186, **0.0210**, 0.0182, 0.0178 |
| draw sd | 3.4% | 2.2% | 7.8% |
| vs default | | 0.893 | 0.843 |

Agent / control 0.944 [0.883, 1.014]: three of the four agent draws are 7-11% better than
every control draw, the fourth is worse than all of them. So the slice probably does help
swe, by about 5%, though it does not narrow the lottery — on this slice it widens it, while
the control's spread is 2.2% once no handful of rows dominates. By language, the gain is
largest in Rust (0.87) and Go (0.92), next to nothing in TypeScript, Java and PHP (0.99-1.04),
and Python and JS 0.96. At 0.20 swe / 0.80 wild the composite still loses about 1.4%, and
which half of the wild cost is dilution remains untested.

The card's swe slice should be the 200-session size: at 40 rows the draw spread is mostly
which few rows were drawn. It takes chunking to score — qbench's streamed bf16 reference
keeps every waiting row's activations on the device, ~18 GB for 2.2M context tokens at 9B,
so four 50-row traces on 16 GB cards.

The direction this leaves: language and format diversity belong in documents (`code.utf8`
or a corpus like it, a data-format kind), and an agent slice, if any, should carry what no
document does — the model's own work pattern across a long task, planning and revising as
tool output arrives, over varied kinds of work rather than one repository-fix pattern. The
scripted loops have the shape but not that: their tool calls are fixed, and the model only
writes the final answer.

## Where a calibration misses: a stress test (2026-10-05)

A calibrated quant minimizes error weighted by the calibration's Hessian, so the error it
saves in covered input directions goes somewhere: into directions the calibration barely
excites (floored at `sigma_reg` = 0.025 x mean diagonal, [quantize.py](../deps/exllamav3/exllamav3/modules/quant/exl3_lib/quantize.py)).
An uncalibrated quant spreads it evenly. Content that lives in those weak directions should
then do *worse* calibrated than uncalibrated. To find out how much worse, and on what, a
stress trace of content chosen to miss the corpus (`quantization/stress/build.py`), scored
on Ornith-1.5-9B 4 bpw: four uncalibrated draws, four of the control mix, three default.

What it says:
- **Most of calibration's gain is not about content.** Even Tibetan, emoji and hex dumps
  keep 20-35% less error than uncalibrated: the Hessian's dominant structure (outlier
  channels, the directions every input excites) is common to all text. The content-specific
  part is the layer on top — 0.52 on prose like the corpus against 0.70-0.80 far from it.
  Distance from the corpus is a gradient of shrinking benefit, not a sign flip.
- **Context length is not a miss.** Positions up to 32k keep the benefit of 2048-token
  calibration rows.
- **The one flip is base64**: 28% worse than uncalibrated, both calibrations alike.
  Near-uniform text over a 64-character alphabet, cut by the tokenizer into odd fragments,
  is neither the corpus nor its random-token rows (uniform over the vocabulary); hex dumps,
  as high-entropy but heavily structured, are fine at 0.67. It is large in relative and
  small in absolute terms (calibrated, 0.033 per token, below prose's 0.038), and not
  content worth calibrating for — but it is a ready worst case whenever one is needed.
- **The stakes are absolute error times how common the content is.** By that, git diffs
  stand out: the highest absolute excess of any realistic content (0.109 per token
  uncalibrated, about 0.08 calibrated, twice prose), a 0.74 ratio, and constant in agent
  work. Typed chat markup is higher still (0.22). SQL's ratio is poor but these rows are a
  package-history dump with tiny absolute error; written schemas and queries are untested.
- Repetition (a token, a short cycle, a sentence, repeated) cannot be read: the model is so
  sure of it that the bf16 noise floor rivals the quants' excess, and the ratios' intervals
  cross zero.

Not run: the per-layer ceiling (Hessian eigenvectors against the dequantized weights) and a
token search for worse than base64; the stakes above did not call for them.

## Each calibration protects the regime it contains

The same comparison across the three calibrations measured, by the kind of input:

| calibration | raw text | the model's own chat | external text inside a conversation | external text as the assistant's reply (render) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| default (raw web text) | all draws equal | all draws equal, match td | draws within ~±5%, up to 17% apart at a single thinking setting | a wide lottery, 0.0098-0.0370 |
| SC (own chat only) | — | best, -25 to -31% | about equal to plain (0.95-1.00) | **5x worse** than plain |
| conversational mix | unchanged at 27B (3-7% worse at 9B) | better, -13 to -18% | better, -14 to -29% | narrow, 0.0095-0.0133 |

The reading that fits all of it: the Hessians only see the activations the calibration
produces, and the quantizer only controls error in the directions they cover. Outside
them, the error is whatever the rounding and the sign draw happen to put there — large,
or small, and different every draw. That is why a *narrow* calibration shows up as
*variance* rather than as a uniform penalty, and why the variance appears only in the
regime nobody covered.

This is also the measured cost of a monotone corpus that
[qbench.md](qbench.md) "In-domain calibration does not survive contact with other data"
predicted: SC's gain on its own distribution is real, and so is a 5x loss where it is
silent. New sources should enter as slices of a diverse mix.

## How we got here: the Qwen3.8 "instability" (2026-09-22 to -24)

It started as a gap: our locally converted Qwen3.8-27B 4bpw scored 2.4x worse than
turboderp's published one, with every input and the converter version identical, while
our 3bpw beat his. Everything below was measured, in the order it cost least to rule out:

- **Not the converter version, resume, or inputs.** v1.4.2 and v1.4.9 produce
  byte-identical weights on one host; a fresh conversion reproduces a resumed one byte for
  byte; base weights, config, tokenizer, template and calibration corpus all match.
- **Not a kernel.** The gated-delta-net chunk kernel for Qwen3.8's 48/16 head shape runs
  at 1.2-1.3x the bf16 floor, identical across hosts, like the 32/16 control. The
  quantized GEMM is equally accurate on a 36-SM and a 70-SM card (bit-identical weights,
  bitwise-different but equally accurate outputs).
- **Not the platform.** Driver 615 vs 610, nvJitLink 13.4 vs 13.2, and a CPU forced to
  baseline dispatch all reproduce the same conversion byte for byte.
- **A lottery, over sign vectors and rounding.** Injecting turboderp's sign vectors into
  our conversion reproduced his quality on one host and not on the other; alias-free
  signs, other seeds and other hosts landed anywhere from 0.0098 to 0.0370. His 4bpw was
  a good draw, fourth of twelve.
- **Not a bad layer.** Swapping bands between a good and a bad same-sign checkpoint put
  the damage in layers 32-47, mostly the MLPs, co-adapted rather than localized; the last
  blocks, where it becomes visible, are irrelevant when swapped.
- **Only in one framing.** Every number above was scored with qbench's
  `template: render`, which frames arbitrary web text as the assistant's reply. On raw
  text the draws agree within 5%. On turboderp's own eval trace — the model's own chat —
  every one of our draws matches his, the worst included. On the conversational slices,
  the muted, realistic version survives only where documents arrive as tool output.

So the "instability" was the default calibration's coverage gap, exposed by an instrument
that happened to aim at it. The fix is the section above.

## Converter internals worth knowing

**Sign vectors alias within a module.** `convert_model.py` seeds each module with its
index and `quantize_exl3` reseeds per tensor with that same value, so any two same-length
draws at the same position produce identical sign vectors. On Qwen3.8 that makes
`gate.sv == down.su` in every layer, `z.sv == out_proj.su`, and `out_proj.sv == down.sv`
— producer/consumer pairs that incoherence processing assumes are independent.
`quantize_exl3_batch` copies the reuse deliberately, to match the serial path. Parallel
mode (`-d 0,1`) races the shared generator and perturbs the pattern run to run, which is
how published checkpoints come to have mixed draw indices. **No quality effect was
measurable** — aliased and alias-free draws overlapped completely (n = 5 and 4) — but it
is wrong on its face, and a per-tensor seed from a hash of the key fixes it without
breaking resume. Queued as a report in [upstream.md](upstream.md).

**A checkpoint's `suh` carries the negated sign of the drawn `su`**
(`su * in_channel_scales / (-codebook_scale)` in `regularize()`); `svh` keeps `sv`'s sign.
Anything that reads or injects signs has to know.

**Nothing in a checkpoint records its calibration.** `in_channel_scales` is the block RMS
of the rotated weight, not the Hessian, so `|suh|` does not fingerprint the calibration,
and `quantization_config.json` records only rows and columns.

**The converter's own per-layer metrics cannot see this class of damage.** They compare
against states on calibration rows, and calibration rows never separated good draws from
bad at any depth. Same-depth comparisons against bf16 on eval rows — relative residual
error, and a logit lens through the bf16 head — were identical across draws through
layer ~50 and separated only near the output. **A bf16 continuation does see it:** run
bf16's own remaining layers on the quantized state at layer L and measure the final KLD.
At L = 48 that ranked six checkpoints in exactly their final order (n = 6). The damage is
latent in the state and amplified by the model's own later layers.

**Determinism.** A single-GPU conversion is byte-exact across runs, across two GPUs of
one model, and across a resume onto a different GPU. Two GPU models give different
rounding and therefore a different draw — not a worse one. Scoring is as stable: driver
610.57.04 -> 615.71.09, and CUDA toolkit 13.4.1 -> 13.4.2 with the extension rebuilt,
left every qbench KL vector bit-identical, the bf16 reference included (2026-10-01).
